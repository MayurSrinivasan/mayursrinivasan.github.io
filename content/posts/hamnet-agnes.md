Watching *Hamnet*, two things caught my ear right away: Shakespeare's wife is called **Agnes** instead of **Anne Hathaway**, and he pronounces it something like *"Agnyes"*.

It turns out both are rooted in how fluid names—and consonants—were in 16th-century England.

In 1581, her father, Richard Hathaway, wrote his will and explicitly left money to **"Agnes, my daughter."** Later church records standardized her as **Anne** on her 1582 marriage bond, so history remembered her as Anne Hathaway. In Elizabethan Stratford, though, *Agnes* and *Anne* weren't treated as two separate names—any more than *Hamnet* and *Hamlet* were for their son. Maggie O'Farrell's novel (and the film) uses *Agnes* to return to the name her own father called her.

As for the pronunciation: the hard modern `G` in *AG-niss* is a relatively recent habit. Under French and ecclesiastical Latin influence (think French *Agnès*), the `gn` softened into a palatal **"ny"** sound—roughly *Ah-nyes* or *Ag-nyes*. In everyday Elizabethan English, it softened even further into **"Annis"**, which is how *Agnes* and *Anne* blurred together in speech in the first place.{{< sidenote >}}It's the same reason *Annis* and *Annys* show up all over 16th-century parish registers—scribes were spelling what they heard.{{< /sidenote >}}

What surprised me most, though, was how familiar *"Agnyes"* sounded to my ear: it hits the exact same tongue position as the **`ज्ञ` (*dnya*)** sound in Marathi (as in *dnyān*, ज्ञान).

That isn't a coincidence. In both Romance languages and Indo-Aryan languages, putting a stop consonant (`g` or `j`) right before a nasal (`n`) is awkward in the back of the mouth, so speakers pulled the whole cluster forward onto the hard palate:

- **Latin → French / Italian:** `gn` became **`ny`** (*Agnès*)
- **Sanskrit `ज्ञ` (*jñ*) → Hindi:** became **`gy`** (*gyān*)
- **Sanskrit `ज्ञ` (*jñ*) → Marathi:** stayed **`dnya`** (*dnyān*), keeping that exact *"ny"* glide

Two branches of Indo-European, centuries apart, solving the same tongue-twister in almost the exact same way—and somehow linking Stratford-upon-Avon to Mumbai in a single syllable.
