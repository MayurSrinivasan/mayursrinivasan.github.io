1. Eleven mornings since 2014

The Berkeley Half Marathon launched in 2013 with a half marathon, a 10-miler, and a 10K. The 5K joined in November 2014 with 1,104 finishers and has been run eleven times since.

Two empty slots interrupt the calendar: 2018 was canceled due to Camp Fire wildfire smoke, and 2020 fell to COVID-19. The deferred 2021 edition ran on February 27, 2022, making 2022 the only year with two Berkeley 5Ks. Turnout peaked in 2019 (1,589) after 2018 rollovers, rebuilt to 1,548 in 2024, and dipped to 1,201 in 2026 under a 100% rain forecast.

2. A remarkably steady median

Across twelve years of shifting field sizes, the typical Berkeley 5K finisher has stayed anchored inside a 1 minute 46 second band.

The fastest median was the inaugural 2014 race at 34:56 (with 2017 close behind at 35:07). Between 2019 and 2023, the median drifted about a minute and a half slower—peaking at 36:42 in 2019 and 36:36 in 2023—before settling back into the mid-35s: 35:32 (2024), 35:52 (2025), and 35:56 (2026).

3. Min vs. max: winners and the cutoff tail

At the very front, the course record still belongs to Dave Winnacker’s 15:33 in 2015. Every winning time since has landed between 16:10 (2022 Nov) and 17:53 (2017)—and the sub-25-minute front pack actually hit an all-time high of 9.4% in 2026.

At the back, the raw maximum tells a story about cutoff enforcement rather than fitness. Most years cap out around 1:10–1:22, just past the one-hour course limit. Three editions—2014 (2:05:51), 2019 (1:45:09), and Feb 2022 (2:11:32)—left the timing mat open longer. Use the toggle above the chart to compare the raw max against the steadier 95th percentile.

4. How dispersion evolved: the right tail stretched

The real structural shift isn’t in the median—it’s in the spread. Between 2014 and 2017, the interquartile range (IQR, middle 50%) stayed razor-tight between 11:32 and 11:52.

Starting in 2019 and peaking in both 2022 races, the IQR stretched past 13:58—nearly two and a half minutes wider. Crucially, the widening was one-sided: the 25th percentile (Q1) barely budged in twelve years (always 29:35–31:01, an 86-second band), while the 75th percentile (Q3) climbed nearly four minutes from 41:10 to 44:59 as walkers and families (45+ min) grew from 15.5% to 25.0% of the field. Since 2024, the IQR has partially re-compressed to ~12:35.

5. Every distribution, stacked in order

Summary lines hide the texture of a race. Plotting the smooth finish-time density for every edition reveals a classic right-skewed community 5K: a sharp runner peak around 30–35 minutes followed by a second shoulder of walkers beyond 45 minutes.

6. Does Berkeley’s November weather move the clock?

At the 8:30 AM start in Downtown Berkeley, every single edition sits inside a remarkably runner-friendly Goldilocks window: 48.0°F to 57.4°F. Because it is never truly hot, temperature shows almost no correlation with median time (r = −0.11).

Wind lines up more closely (r = +0.65): the crispest, calmest modern morning—2024 (48.1°F, 3.6 mph, 56% RH)—produced the fastest post-2017 median (35:32). Only 2016 saw steady drizzle during the 8:30–9:30 AM window, while 2026 was the warmest and windiest start on record (57.4°F, 11.1 mph), ahead of an afternoon storm whose 100% rain forecast trimmed turnout by 20%—leaving a faster-skewed field on the start line.

7. Where I land on the curve

I ran my first Berkeley 5K in 2025 in 34:50 (11:13/mi)—placing #672 of 1,502, faster than 55.3% of the field (top 44.7%) and 1:02 ahead of the median.

A year later in 2026, despite the warmest and windiest morning in the race’s history, I trimmed 36 seconds to finish in 34:14 (11:01/mi)—#510 of 1,201, faster than 57.5% of finishers (top 42.5%; #309/550 Male; #56/106 M30–39).
